\thesisabstract{%
Snow falls in forests over 23\% of the global landmass, where interception by forest canopies plays a crucial role in the terrestrial water cycle and land-atmosphere energy exchanges. Global changes in climate and vegetation cover have the potential to alter how snowfall is partitioned in forested basins worldwide; however, the understanding required to investigate potential changes to water resources and energy exchanges is currently limited. In cold continental climates, up to half of annual snowfall may be lost to the atmosphere through canopy snow sublimation, and in warmer climates, higher melt rates cause a large fraction of snowfall to reach the ground as liquid. Snow interception processes exhibit a strong dependence on both meteorology and canopy density, leading to differing process emergence in existing theories developed in these distinct environments and limited transferability to new regions. Consequently, simulations of the canopy snow mass and energy balance have demonstrated variable accuracy across different climates and canopy structures, hindering the representation of processes in hydrological and land-surface models. Moreover, diagnosing the processes that partition snowfall in needleleaf forests remains uncertain because the underlying snow interception and ablation theories have not been adequately tested. The aim of this research was to evaluate the suitability of existing theories of snow interception and canopy snow ablation and link improved process understanding to model development and validation across a broad range of canopy structures and meteorological conditions. Expanding measurements of snow interception and canopy snow ablation processes across broader environmental gradients, while also better isolating individual processes, would help clarify the applicability of existing theories and guide improvements to enhance model accuracy across diverse sites. 

New observations of initial snow interception, collected across a range of canopy densities and meteorological conditions when ablation processes were minimal, showed that canopy density is the primary factor influencing subcanopy snow throughfall. Contrary to existing theories, no relationships were found with canopy snow load or air temperature and initial interception. Canopy density was best represented by the snow-leaf contact area, which accounts for increasing contact area with more horizontal snowfall trajectories. This metric was highly sensitive to wind speed in sparse canopies, increasing by a factor of 2 with a 1 m s$^{-1}$ wind speed, which also led to a similar reduction in throughfall. These observations show how differing processes emerge at larger scales as canopy elements interact, and demonstrate that traditional throughfall calculations based on binary canopy coverage data will fail in sparse canopies. To better represent this process, a new parameterisation was developed that calculates throughfall as a function of snowfall and snow-leaf contact area, which improved the simulation of throughfall across a range of canopy densities and meteorologies. This approach is consistent with several rainfall interception studies that also separate canopy loading and ablation processes and compute interception as a function of canopy cover. However, it extends these existing approaches by accounting for changes in the snow-leaf contact area associated with the snowfall trajectory angle.

An evaluation of existing theories representing canopy snow unloading was conducted using observations collected across a wide range of meteorological conditions and with more direct measurements of unloading than those used in previous studies. A new model that incorporates canopy snow unloading as a function of canopy snow load, wind shear stress, and melt—coupled with a novel canopy snow energy and mass balance in a hydrological model—improved simulation of canopy snow ablation relative to previous approaches. While existing models have identified canopy load, wind, and melt as key predictors of unloading, they performed less well due to incomplete representation of both dry-snow and melt-driven unloading processes. This resulted either from not including the wind-driven unloading process or from approximating canopy snow melt and associated unloading as a function of air- or ice-bulb temperature. The new relationships provide a stronger physical basis by representing wind-driven unloading through shear stress and melt-induced unloading as a function of energy balance-based canopy snowmelt. Moreover, the ratio of solid snow unloading to liquid meltwater during canopy snowmelt was found to be a function of snow load, building on previous work that used a constant ratio. These new canopy snow mass and energy parameterisations show potential to improve partitioning of intercepted snowfall in needleleaf forest canopies in hydrological and land-surface models.

An uncalibrated validation of the revised canopy snow mass and energy balance parameterisations was conducted at four needleleaf forests characterised by differing tree species, canopy structure, and meteorological conditions. At the one site where observations of canopy snow load were available, the revised model reduced errors in simulated canopy load and in the duration of canopy snow cover. Additionally, the new model lowered errors in simulating subcanopy snow water equivalent (SWE) compared to an existing model across all four sites. The largest reduction in error was observed at the warm-humid maritime site and was attributed to better representation of canopy snowmelt and the melt-induced unloading process. Improved process representation also enabled clearer process-level diagnosis of vegetation's influence on snow accumulation. At two cold, low-snowfall sites, roughly half of the annual snowfall was lost to the atmosphere via sublimation of intercepted snow. A cold, wind-exposed site with higher annual snowfall had higher unloading rates, which reduced canopy snow sublimation. In contrast, at a temperate-maritime site, nearly half of the annual snowfall melted within the canopy and, combined with melt-induced unloading, partitioned the most snowfall towards the ground of all four sites.

This research reveals variability in snowfall interception and canopy snow ablation processes across diverse canopy densities and meteorologies, and shows that this variability is not adequately represented in existing models. New parameterisations of canopy snow mass and energy balance, addressing these limitations, are introduced to more explicitly represent the processes governing snowfall partitioning by needleleaf canopies. This new approach led to more accurate simulations of canopy snow load and subcanopy SWE across a broad range of meteorologies and forest types and also improved process diagnosis, though further validation is required across a diverse range of sites.
